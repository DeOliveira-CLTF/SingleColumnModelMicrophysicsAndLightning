[40] When comparing the predicted flash rate from the
2 km and 500 m horizontal resolution simulations to the
1 km resolution simulation, we get generally the same result
(Figure 6). That is, the flash rate predicted by the precipitation
ice mass ranges from 35 to 75 fl. min−1 with a higher flash
rate during the supercell stage. The flash rate predicted by the
ice mass flux product remains between 20 and 30 fl. min−1.
Flash rates predicted by ice water path, maximum vertical
velocity, cloud top height, and updraft volume are all similar
to the 1 km results. Thus, for simulations with different
horizontal grid resolution of a severe, shear‐driven storm,
these empirical relationships are fairly robust.
[41] In comparing the predicted flash rate from the different
microphysics parameterizations (Lin, 1983, Thompson, 2004,
and N4r4) simulations, we find only a little variability
(Figure 6). The flash rates predicted by the precipitation ice
mass show some variability, depending on the scheme, with
N4r4 microphysics generally producing flash rates less than
the other two schemes. The maximum flash rate estimated
from the precipitation ice mass using the N4r4 microphysics
scheme only reaches 65 fl. min−1, while it exceeds 75 fl.
min−1 when the Lin, 1983 and Thompson, 2004 schemes are
used. The flash rates inferred by the cloud top height, the
updraft volume, and the flux product are similar in magnitude
in all three microphysics parameterizations. The flash rate
predicted by the maximum updraft speed (PR92) is similar in
magnitude to the Lin, 1983 scheme for both the Thompson,
2004 and N4r4 schemes. However, the Thompson, 2004
simulation has a trend similar to observations in that the
flash rate increases during the supercell stage. When com-
paring the flash rate predicted by the BB08 equation using
the Thompson, 2004 microphysics parameterization, there is
a distinct increase in flash rate during the supercell stage
(from 5 to 30 fl. min−1). The flash rates are still lower than
observed (30 fl. min−1 versus 47 fl. min−1), but tuning the
0.19 factor would likely bring the predicted flash rate into
agreement with observations. In conclusion, the chosen
microphysics scheme does not seem to produce significant
differences in predicted lightning flash rate as long as the
storm is reasonably represented.
